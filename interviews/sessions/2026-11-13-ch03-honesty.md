# Interview session — 2026-11-13 (second session)

- **Chapter:** 3, Honesty (*Hide Nothing*)
- **Topic:** where the value of honesty comes from; what a dishonest language
  looks like; what honesty in a language opens up
- **Interviewer:** ghostwriter skill
- **Chosen because:** chapters 2–12 have no interview coverage; honesty is the
  principle behind the other nine, and the first session surfaced it as the
  author's core value ("I care about honesty and truth").

---

**Q:** Silica's home page sets an unusual goal: to be the world's most
intellectually honest, secure language and runtime. Last session you said
advocacy isn't what you're about; you care about honesty and truth. Before
Silica, what did a *dishonest* language or tool look like in your own working
life? Take me to a moment when one told you one thing and did another.

**A:** The realization happened when I first began to understand what compiler optimizers were actually doing. Yes they would remove noops and code duplication, but what they spent most of their time doing was converting PDP-11 behaviors into modern chip behaviors. That disturbed me. It was especially disturbing to hear otherwise knowlegeable people talk about languages with these optimizers be referred to as 'close to the metal.' Those languages just weren't and aren't.

**Q:** When did that understanding arrive? Were you reading a paper, looking at
generated assembly, teaching a compilers course? Take me to the point where
you first saw the optimizer translating an old machine into a new one.

**A:** There is an article called, "C is not a low-level language" published by the ACM. That was the first time I had language I could use to express my nagging concern.

> Interviewer note: the article is David Chisnall, "C Is Not a Low-level
> Language: Your computer is not a fast PDP-11," ACM Queue 16(2), 2018.
> Verify citation before printing. Quoting it in the body is allowed (not the
> author's words).

**Q:** Silica's answer to that seems to run in two directions. It refuses to let
the compiler quietly repair code (the rules that make "the optimizer will fix
it" patterns into errors), and it tries to describe the real chip instead of
the PDP-11. Did Chisnall's article lead straight to those decisions, or did
the connection come later, when you were already designing Silica?

**A:** His ideas had been at the front of my brain since I read it when published. I just didn't have tools to do anything of any worth and size about it.

**Q:** Seven years with an idea at the front of your mind and no way to act on it.
What were the tools you lacked? And what changed by 2025 that finally gave
you enough to build something "of worth and size"?

**A:** LLM and the scaling they allow. Even from the inside it seems to be a huge lift has been accomplished by using the LLMs. Not to vibe-code, but to act as my 'army of developers on my team' as one collegue has referred to my use of them. For example, when I was working on getting SwErl to communicate with BEAM nodes, it took well over a month to make progress. With the LLMs and the Erlang/BEAM documentation and LLMs, it took 20 minutes under my guidance.

**Q:** That raises an honesty question a careful reader will ask, so I'd rather
ask it first. Silica's AI policy says the project doesn't accept AI as a
co-author on commits and that all work is the responsibility of the human who
submits it. Yet a large share of Silica's own commits carry an AI co-author
line. How do you square the policy with the record, and how should the book
describe your "army of developers" honestly?

**A:** Initially I didn't know how much trust could be placed in LLM generated code. It was and is hard to tell the hype, for and against, from the truth until you use them to do somthing significant. Also, the models and the tooling have improved over the time Silica has been under development. Additionally, as there was more Silica compiler code written, it was easier for the LLMs to use that code when looking for patterns to apply.

**Q:** So your trust in the models grew as they improved and as there was more
Silica for them to learn from. Let me press on the policy itself, since this
chapter is about honesty: does the AI policy, as written, still say what you
believe? If you were rewriting it today, would the line about co-authors
change, or is it the commit record that's out of step?

**A:** the policy is out of date. The trust has to shift to the contributor who is using the LLM tool. I'm still thinking about how to state that in a policy.

**Q:** Let me turn to what honesty makes possible, which is the heart of this
chapter. Suppose a Silica program really does tell the truth about itself:
its effects, its memory, its foreign code, its failures. What can someone do
with a program like that which they can't do with a C or Java program today?
And who do you picture benefiting first?

**A:** It is easier for LLMs to reason about it, find bugs in it, and extend it. Silica is designed to be LLM friendly and human readable. When I'm working on the compiler, BEES, etc, I don't use a text editor nor an IDE. I will at times use vim to look at code where things could use a detailed look, but I have not needed those 'programmer-help applications' kinds of appliations. That means my cognative load is lowered since I don't have to learn commands for the tools.

**Q:** That's what honesty gives the person building the program. What about
someone who didn't write it and has to trust it: a security reviewer, an
auditor, a regulator, a student inheriting the code? What changes for them
when the program can't hide its effects, memory, foreign code or failures?

**A:** the dangerous_* indicator, is a big step towards helping those people. If there is bad code in their application and bugs are being expressed, the probability is very high that's where the problem lies. These trees of dangerous code declared clearly are a huge help. nothing hidden is the principle. Knowing what the data type is is also a huge advantage. If the code you see is the code that runs, it is easier to reason about, analyze, and debug.

**Q:** Honesty also caught Silica itself. In October 2026 the project found that
the runtime had been managing long-lived memory through a heap nobody had
decided to build, while the public pages said memory lived on actor stacks.
How did you find out, and what went through your mind when you realised your
own language had been hiding something from you?

**A:** it was an 'oh...crap' moment. I had been pushing really hard to get behavior I needed and hadn't been paying enough attention to what my army of highly focused, yet unscupulous, workers had been producing. it was time to get back in deeply and get it fixed.

**Q:** "Highly focused, yet unscrupulous" is a vivid way to describe your army.
After that moment, what did you change in how you direct and check them, so
that nothing like the hidden heap could slip past you again?

**A:** I now do more reviews. automatted where I can. Asking for multiple checks against the design documents.
