# Interview session — 2026-11-14 (thirteenth session)

- **Chapter:** 1, Why? and Why Not? (second session)
- **Topic:** the 24 December scene; the fate of the Rust BEAM port; the most
  uncomfortable *why?*; the hardest reversal; an epigraph
- **Interviewer:** ghostwriter skill
- **Chosen because:** every chapter now has a session; Chapter 1 holds the most
  remaining gaps, including the book's pivotal scene.

---

**Q:** On 24 December 2025, where were you when you stopped working on the Rust
port? What was on your screen or on paper? And was it Christmas Eve in the family
sense, or an ordinary working day?

(The session paused here while the author asked for a preface to be written;
the question was put again afterwards.)

**A:** it was Christmas Eve day. I'm older so it was just me and my spouse at home because our children were with their families. I needed something to keep me occupied.

**Q:** What part of the port were you working on when you paused — scheduler,
garbage collector, native-function interface, something else? Did a particular
piece make the square-peg problem suddenly obvious?

**A:** adding the JIT compiler and working through the memory issues using it produced.

**Q:** A JIT writes machine code into memory while the program runs, exactly where
Rust's guarantees give way to `unsafe`. What were the memory issues? Was there a
moment, working through them, when you saw the port couldn't deliver the
memory-safe virtual machine it was meant to?

**A:** The OS kept moving the allocations that the JIT compiler was attempting to use. It was anoying, but fixable. The JIT compiler had to be called from unsafe Rust. I wouldn't call it the trigger. It was just data. The 'wrong problem' came into my mind without bidding.

> Interviewer note: the interviewer's framing (JIT as the trigger) was declined.
> Present the JIT issues as data, and the insight as arriving unbidden.

**Q:** What became of the port afterwards? Is the code still somewhere, set aside,
or did Silica make it unnecessary? Did anything from it carry into Silica?

**A:** Silica made it unnecessery. I didn't touch it again.

**Q:** Sometimes asking why turns up a good reason. Which "why?" was hardest to
ask — the one where the conventional answer proved strongest, or where you kept
something you'd expected to throw out?

**A:** Neither. Why is never hard to ask. We may not like the answer, but the question isn't hard to ask. Which answer is the hardest? Neither. Answers are just data.

**Q:** Chapter 1 has no epigraph, and under the quotation rule it can't be your
own words. Is there a line from someone else that has stayed with you and
captures asking why and then why not?

**A:** If you find one of my quotes that would be appropriate you can use it.

> Interviewer note: author grants an exception to the quotation rule for the
> Chapter 1 epigraph. Recorded in style.md.
