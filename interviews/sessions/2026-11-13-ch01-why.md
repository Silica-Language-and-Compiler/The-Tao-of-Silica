# Interview session — 2026-11-13

- **Chapter:** 1, Why? and Why Not? (with voice decision)
- **Topic:** settling the book's voice; the origin of the first "why?"
- **Interviewer:** ghostwriter skill
- **Chosen because:** first session; no chapter has interview coverage, and
  Chapter 1 is the book's spine. Open threads: the first "why?", and the gap
  between BEAM_SEMP's last commit and Silica's first.

**Q:** Before we start on content, one decision shapes everything I write: should
the book be in your own first person, as a ghostwritten book usually is, or in
the third person, with a narrator describing you and your work? The drafts
currently use a neutral third person ("Barney") so they can go either way.

**A:** third person

**Q:** Your 2024 outline for "Unsafe Impedance" opens by citing two documents:
the White House technical report and CISA's case for memory-safe roadmaps. The
finished paper asks why a language earns the label "memory safe" when it still
lets unsafe code be written, loaded and hidden. Was reading those reports the
first time that question came to you, or had it been forming earlier? If
earlier, take me to the first moment you remember asking it.

**A:** I don't remember the first time. I remember when Java was new how much safer it felt than the C and C++ I had been writing in industry. When I read those articles, I had to wonder how they came up with that list of languages. Why were those the ones selected? I was not the initiator of the idea to write the paper. I was at a professional, not academic conference, called CodeBEAM in San Francisco when I was approached by a member of the EEF to write an article that, if the data was there, would argue that BEAM languages should have been included in the list.

**Q:** So the paper began as a case for the BEAM: show that Erlang and Elixir
belonged on the agencies' list. Your March outline still reads that way, with
sections on how Erlang and Elixir avoid each class of error. But the published
paper turns. It concedes that native functions let even BEAM code hide unsafe
behaviour, rates Erlang and Elixir only at the low end of "high" impedance, and
ends by saying no secure-by-default language with infinite impedance exists.
When did you realise the data was taking you somewhere other than where the
paper was asked to go, and what did that feel like?

**A:** I felt fine with it. Advocacy is not what I'm about. I care about honesty and truth. I could not honestly state the BEAM languages were perfect either, nor can I say that about Silica. I really didn't want to include Fifi in Silica, but in order to allow for reasonable switch over frome very dangerous languages on boards an when writing operationg systems, I had to add it.

**Q:** Silica's founding notes in December 2025 said flatly: no C interop. By
May 2026 Fifi existed. You've just said you didn't want it, and that it came
down to letting people move over from dangerous languages on boards and in
operating systems. Who were you picturing when you made that decision — a
particular kind of team, a project, a conversation — and what would have
happened to them if Silica had held the line?

**A:** I was picturing people I know, academics and professionals, that write code that runs on boards and raw computer. Those boards are everywhere. They control our cars, our electrical and water systems, our networks, and our daily-life computer interactions. I felt to the point of almost knowing that, given the very long history with C and their propensity to think of the hardware in terms of the PDP-11 model, that it would be a very hard, long bit of work to even get them to look at a language other than C. So far that has been true for most. I knew that offering the Fifi escape hatch would make it easier for them to adopt Silica.

**Q:** You said that so far it has been true for most. Tell me about the ones it
wasn't true for. Who has actually looked at Silica from the C and bare-metal
world, and what made them willing to look?

**A:** What interests them the most is being able to express the reality of the device directly. Each individual raw computer or board has chips that expose behaviors they had not been able to access without great difficulty.

**Q:** Give me one concrete case: a particular board or chip, and a behaviour on
it that someone could finally express directly in Silica after struggling to
reach it in C. What was the behaviour, and what did they say or do when they
saw it?

**A:** the different memory types, the audited pointers, those types of things. Being able to say that the chip enforces atomic memory or one of the other memmory types seems to resonate with them.

**Q:** Let me take you back to the turn from "why?" to "why not?". BEAM_SEMP's
last commits were in October 2025: two months spent pulling dangerous defaults
out of a running BEAM. Silica's first commit is 26 December 2025, a bootstrap
compiler for AArch64. What happened in those weeks in between? Was there a day,
a conversation or a failure when you decided to stop patching and build a
language?

**A:** I had been working concurrently on a Rust port of the BEAM. I was tired of saying Erlang is a memory safe language only to have people ask what the virtual machine was written in. I had to reply truthfully with 'C'. Then they would claim it couldn't really be safe then, could it. I was working on the Rust port, and had made huge progress. I was well along the path to completion of the re-engineering of the VM on the 24th of December. I was working on it when I paused and did some thinking and pondering. It came to me that I was solving the wrong problem. I then tried for a few minutes to find a language from the many I know and have used, that could express the SwErl, SEMP, ideas readily and still be memory safe, which Rust could not do well. It took me only a few minutes to realize there was no such language. The initial on-paper and in-head designs were done in a few hours. One other thing. I had what is colloquially called a mini-stroke in June of 2025. When that happened, I couldn't read. Thinking was difficult. Thinking about big and complex things was not possible, and my ability to form sentences so I could speak was heavily restricted. The ongoing Rust re-engineering allowed me to regain some of my original ability. Eventually I was able to think at a level which enabled me to recognize I was solving the wrong problem. Now I've been blessed through these efforts to be learning Tagalog. Language is language; human or computer.

**Q:** Thank you for telling me about the stroke. Before I write a word of it: is

**A:** its not a secret. We have to tell our stories so when similar things happen to others they know they aren't alone; not the only one who has faced the problem.

**Q:** Back to 24 December. You paused over the Rust port and saw that you were
solving the wrong problem. If porting the BEAM to Rust was the wrong problem,
what was the right one? Put it the way you would have put it to yourself that
day.

**A:** Every language is designed to solve a certain set of problems. The problem was trying to find a way to force a language that wasn't designed to express the BEAM ideas and principles to express them: round holes, square pegs. There was no way the solution created by any language I knew of would produce a clean solution, using engineering speak.

**Q:** That is the "why?". Now the "why not?". Realising that no language fits
is one thing; deciding to build one yourself, six months after a stroke, with a
Rust port nearly finished, is another. Most people would have finished the port
and lived with the square peg. What made you think "why not build it" rather
than "someone should build it"?

**A:** I had been leaning on LLMs to do the coding I could not do at the time due to the stroke. I realized that they struggled with the existing languages because those languages were written for human-only understanding. That was one reason why it was so hard to get the LLMs to give the code I wanted to produce. I realized it may just be easier to start from scratch and build a language and compiler that would leverage what LLMs could do rather than fighting them to get them to do someting they aren't good at. Language is language. all languages, even LLMs, are designed for certaing problems to be solved.

> Interviewer note: resolves the open question of whether the LLM-friendly
> principle preceded building Silica with agents: it came from relying on LLMs
> for coding during stroke recovery, before Silica existed.

**Q:** Take me to the struggle itself. When you were leaning on LLMs to write the
Rust port, what did they get wrong over and over? Is there a particular kind of
mistake you watched them make that turned directly into one of Silica's rules?

**A:** They rally struggled with dealing with user named types of any significant depth where named types included named types. By analogy, I realized that this would also be true for deep inheritance trees.

**Q:** Generics went six days after the first commit, on 31 December 2025, with
a commit message that said "for now". Was that the same lesson, models losing
their way in type parameters the way they did in nested names, or a different
reason? And is "for now" still the plan, or has it become permanent?

**A:** Generics are intended to reduce the complicatedness of computation. Unfortunately, when theory hit the real world they did not. They kind of became a fad. If you language didn't have generics, people thought it couldn't be a real language. I'm not sure that reducing complicatedness holds true. It hasn't in my case. I'm sure there are plenty of others, if they had experience outside of languages with generics, would also agree. Being able to see the data-shape your working with allows you to reason about the code more realistically.

**Q:** So the "for now" has become a conviction. One wrinkle a careful reader
will spot: Silica's standard library does take type parameters (an ordered
map, for instance, is told the kinds of keys and values it holds). How do you
see that as different from generics?

**A:** I see traits as being different that generics. Traits were around long before generics and OO classes. They have a different feel to them and they are different enough so that they are not confused with generics by prammers when they are first introduced to them.
