# Interview session — 2026-11-14 (twelfth session)

- **Chapter:** 2, How We Got Here
- **Topic:** the bootstrap in Rust; the pace against the plan; throwing work
  away; the quiet summer of 2026; the trials and the training pairs; the
  October reckoning
- **Interviewer:** ghostwriter skill
- **Chosen because:** the only chapter without a session of its own.

---

**Q:** On 26 December 2025, two days after realising the Rust BEAM port was the
wrong problem, the first Silica commit was a bootstrap compiler written in Rust.
You've compared Rust's memory rules to producing a musical with a young,
unstable cast. Why build Silica's first compiler in Rust, of all languages?

**A:** I had been heavily working in Rust, already had the Rust tooling installed, and all I really needed was a minimal compiler that could build a compiler that could build itself. I didn't need all of Silica's design express in the Rust compiler's output.

**Q:** The founding strategy budgeted six to nine months for the Rust/LLVM
bootstrap. By mid-January you were planning the Silica-written compiler, and on
16 February it compiled its first program. What made it so much faster than
planned, and what did you skip?

**A:** I skipped nothing. I was able to make very rapid progress due to Silica's design, the flexibility of my development approach, and my knowlegeable use of LLMs.

**Q:** "Flexibility of my development approach" could mean many things. What did
a working day on Silica look like in those first weeks? How did you split the
work between yourself and the models, and what was flexible about it compared
with how you'd built software in industry?

**A:** Look at the commit histories we've communicated about before. Build it; measure it; replace it with better. That's different from industries 'If it aint broke don't fix it' approach. radically different. As described earlier, the LLMs did the typing, I did the thinking and evaluating.

**Q:** On 29 June 2026 you deleted the entire first data-structure library, about
a month of work: "overly complicated for purpose". What did the measuring show?
Was throwing it away easy, given what you've said about sunk costs, or did it
sting?

**A:** It used too much memory, ran too slow, and didn't capture the hopes I had for their use. It was sad to toss it, but felt good to start over hanving learned what not to do. I anticipate it could happen again.

**Q:** Through July the compiler was converted to build itself; machines kept
running out of memory, and the last blocker was a pointer into the compiler's own
table of names that decoded as the text "ciency\0:". Was there a low point that
summer, and what kept you going?

**A:** It was a little frustrating, but each small change bringing seeable results let me know I was on the right track. Headed in a good direction.

**Q:** In September you asked for about 20,000 paired trials "for LLM training".
Generating them turned up silent miscompilations (the interviewer garbled the
record's example, `20 + (x + 1)` giving 41). Was finding bugs part of the plan?
And what is the training set for — teaching models to write Silica?

**A:** Finding bugs is always part of the plan. No code is perfect. All code is flawed. All code will always be flawed. It is no reflection on the worth of the person producing the code. Yes, the intent of them was to train models. The intent was also to find edge cases that hadn't been dealt with.

> Interviewer note: the miscompilation example as given in the research notes is
> ambiguous (41 is correct if x = 20). Check the source addendum before using
> any example in the book; otherwise describe it only as silent miscompilation.

**Q:** The draft says that after the hidden per-actor heap was discovered, some
forty design decisions were made in three days and everything moved onto actor
stacks, with release proven by the compiler. That account is partly from the
agents' notes. Is "forty decisions in three days" accurate, and how did those
days feel from the inside?

**A:** Sounds about right. I remember those days as exciting.
